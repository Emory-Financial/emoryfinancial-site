Buying a Home Soon? Read This Before You Open a Store Card or Use Buy Now, Pay Later This Black Friday

By Derek Shao, NMLS 242117 · November 22, 2026 · 7 min read

The short answer: If you're applying for a mortgage, already approved, or in escrow, hold off on new credit this holiday season: store cards, financed furniture or electronics, and big balances on existing cards. Lenders look at your credit again before closing, and if a new debt shows up, Fannie Mae's rules require the lender to recalculate your debt-to-income ratio, which can change or even end an approval. A single new inquiry usually costs less than five FICO points, but the new monthly payment is often the bigger problem. If you need something big for the new house, buy it after you close.

As of November 22, 2026. Holiday spending figures are Adobe's national forecast, not Emory Financial data.

Why does this matter this week?

Because the offers are everywhere. Adobe forecasts a record $275.1 billion in U.S. online holiday spending from November 1 to December 31, up 6.7% from last year, with Black Friday (November 27) at $12.9 billion and Cyber Monday (November 30) at $15.1 billion. It expects shoppers to put $21.3 billion of that on buy now, pay later plans in November and December (Adobe, September 28, 2026).

None of that is a problem on its own. The problem is timing. A lot of my California buyers are pre-approved right now and planning to write offers in January, or they're in escrow trying to close before the new year. For them, a "save 20% today when you open a card" offer at checkout is a decision about their mortgage, not just about a TV.

I'm already approved. Can a new card or loan still affect my mortgage?

Yes. Approval isn't the finish line, and lenders check.

Fannie Mae, whose rules cover a large share of conventional loans, is direct about it: "If a borrower takes on new debt before closing on their mortgage loan without telling their lender, it can potentially make the loan ineligible for purchase by Fannie Mae or trigger a post-purchase review" (Fannie Mae, Undisclosed Liabilities).

Here's what the rules say happens when a lender finds new debt:

  • Your ratios get redone. If a new debt is discovered "after the underwriting decision has been made, up to and concurrent with closing, the lender must recalculate the borrower's debt-to-income ratio" (Fannie Mae Selling Guide B3-6-01).
  • The file goes back through underwriting. Lenders "must follow all Selling Guide requirements to recalculate the debt-to-income (DTI) and resubmit the loan" to Fannie Mae's automated system when the change is big enough.
  • You'll be asked to explain. A debt on your credit report that isn't on your application calls for "a reasonable explanation for the undisclosed debt."
  • Inquiries show up. Credit reports for a mortgage must list every inquiry from the previous 90 days (Fannie Mae Selling Guide B3-5.2-01), so a store card application last week is visible even before the account reports.

Loans also have to close before the credit report used to approve them expires. If a holiday delay pushes your closing past that date, the credit has to be updated, and the new report will show whatever you've added since. I'm using Fannie Mae's rules here because they're public and detailed. FHA, VA and jumbo lenders have their own versions, so ask yours.

How much does a new credit card or store card hurt my score?

Usually less than people fear. FICO says that "for most people, one additional credit inquiry will take less than five points off their FICO Scores," and inquiries only count toward your score for 12 months, though they stay on your report for two years (myFICO).

New credit is only 10% of a FICO score. Amounts owed is 30%, which is why running up a big balance on a card you already have can do more damage than one new application (myFICO, What's in your credit score).

Points still matter near a pricing tier. If your score sits right at a cutoff, a few points can change your loan pricing. For more on which scores lenders use and how that's changing, see my article on VantageScore, FICO and your mortgage credit score.

Does buy now, pay later count against me?

It can, and it's the one people forget to mention. Fannie Mae's guide doesn't have a separate rule for buy now, pay later, so lenders apply the regular rules for whatever the plan looks like on your credit report or bank statements (Fannie Mae Selling Guide B3-6-05):

  • Installment debt must be counted in your debt-to-income ratio "if there are more than ten monthly payments remaining." Shorter debts can still be counted if they significantly affect your ability to pay.
  • Deferred payments, like "no payments until 2027" financing, still count. Fannie Mae says deferred installment debt "must be included" in your monthly obligations.
  • Revolving balances, like a new store card, count at the minimum payment. If no minimum payment is reported, the lender must use 5% of the balance.

A hypothetical, not a quote: put $3,000 of furniture on a new store card that hasn't reported a minimum payment yet, and the lender may have to count $150 a month against you. For a buyer who's already close to the limit, that can be the difference.

Your lender will also look at your bank statements, where recurring buy now, pay later payments show up even if the plan never reaches your credit report. Tell your loan officer about every plan you have open, even the small ones.

What should I do instead?

A few more rules of thumb:

  • Don't move big money around without a paper trail. Large deposits and transfers during escrow raise questions too. If family is giving you gift money for the down payment, see my article on how gift timing works.
  • Don't close old cards to "clean up" your credit. That can shrink your available credit and push your balances up as a share of your limits.
  • Keep paying everything on time. Payment history is 35% of your FICO score. A missed payment during a busy holiday season does far more harm than any one inquiry.

If you're closing in December, my guide to closing on a home in December covers the holiday dates and property tax deadlines.

Frequently asked questions

Can I open a credit card after I'm pre-approved for a mortgage?

You can, but it's best to wait until after closing. Lenders check credit again before closing, and under Fannie Mae's rules any new debt they find means your debt-to-income ratio has to be recalculated, which can change your approval.

Does buy now, pay later affect getting a mortgage?

It can. Fannie Mae's guide has no separate rule for it, so lenders count it like any other debt: installment plans with more than ten payments left must be counted, and deferred-payment plans count too. Recurring payments can also show up on your bank statements. Tell your lender about every plan you have.

How many points does a credit inquiry take off my score?

FICO says that for most people, one additional inquiry takes less than five points off. Inquiries count toward your FICO score for 12 months and stay on your report for two years.

Will my lender pull my credit again before closing?

It often does, and the loan has to close before the credit report used for approval expires. Fannie Mae also has lenders check for new debt during the process and in quality reviews after closing, so it's safest to assume any new account will be seen.

What if I already opened a store card?

Tell your loan officer right away and send the account details. A small new account is often fine, but your lender needs to recalculate your ratios before closing, not discover it at the last minute.
