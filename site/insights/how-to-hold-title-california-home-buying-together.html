Buying a Home With a Spouse, Partner, Parent or Sibling? How You Hold Title in California Matters More Than You Think

By Derek Shao, NMLS 242117 · November 24, 2026 · 7 min read

The short answer: In California, the way you "vest" title on your deed decides what happens to the house if one owner dies, and it can change the tax bill when it's eventually sold. Married couples and registered domestic partners usually choose between community property, community property with right of survivorship, and joint tenancy. Unmarried co-buyers, like a parent and adult child or two siblings, usually choose joint tenancy or tenants in common. Escrow will ask you this question, often with little time to think about it, so it's worth deciding before you're signing.

As of November 24, 2026. This is general education, not legal or tax advice. An estate planning attorney or tax professional should confirm the right choice for your family.

Why does how I hold title matter?

Thanksgiving is when a lot of families start talking about buying together: a couple combining finances, parents helping an adult child, siblings pooling money in a high-cost county. The question that comes up at the very end of that process, usually from the escrow officer, is "How do you want to take title?"

Your answer goes on the deed that's recorded with the county. It controls three things people tend to care about later:

  • Who gets the house if one owner dies, and whether that happens automatically or through the courts.
  • The tax basis the survivor inherits, which can make a big difference in capital gains tax if the home is later sold.
  • Whether a change on title triggers a property tax reassessment or affects your mortgage.

What are the main ways to hold title in California?

Community property. For married couples. The IRS notes that in community property states, including California, "married individuals are each usually considered to own half the community property" (IRS Publication 555). There's no automatic right of survivorship, so each spouse's half passes by will or trust, and without a trust the surviving spouse may need a court step such as a Spousal Property Petition (Orange County Superior Court).

Community property with right of survivorship. Available for deeds created on or after July 1, 2001. The deed has to expressly say it, and the owners accept it in writing on the document. When one spouse dies, the property passes to the survivor "without administration," using the same procedures as joint tenancy (California Civil Code 682.1). The surviving spouse can generally record an affidavit with a certified death certificate, with no court order needed (Sacramento County Public Law Library).

Joint tenancy. Two or more people own equal shares, created by a single deed or will that expressly declares it a joint tenancy (Civil Code 683). When one owner dies, the property passes to the surviving joint tenants without probate. The survivor records an Affidavit of Death of Joint Tenant with a certified death certificate, plus a Preliminary Change of Ownership Report (Orange County Superior Court).

Tenants in common. This is California's default when people other than a married couple buy together and the deed doesn't say otherwise (Civil Code 686). Each owner's share passes under their own will or trust, not to the other owners. That's often what siblings, friends or a parent and child actually want, especially when they contributed different amounts.

A living trust sits on top of all of this. Many families take title in their own names at closing and then move the home into a revocable trust. I covered how that works with your mortgage in my living trust article.

Why do married couples care so much about community property?

Because of the tax basis. When someone inherits a home, the basis is generally reset to the value at the date of death. For community property, IRS Publication 551 says that when either spouse dies, the total value of the community property, "even the part belonging to the surviving spouse," generally becomes the basis of the entire property, as long as at least half of it is included in the deceased spouse's gross estate (IRS Publication 551).

Joint tenancy between spouses works differently. The IRS treats it as a "qualified joint interest," includes only half the value in the deceased spouse's estate, and the surviving spouse keeps their original cost basis on their own half (IRS Publication 551).

For a couple who bought decades ago, the gap between those two outcomes can be large when the survivor later sells. How the survivorship version of community property is treated for basis is a question to put to your tax professional before you sign.

Does putting a family member on title trigger a property tax reassessment?

It depends on who and how. According to the California State Board of Equalization:

  • Adding a spouse or registered domestic partner to the deed is excluded from reassessment.
  • Adding a friend or sibling as a joint tenant doesn't trigger reassessment "so long as you, as the original joint tenant, remain as one of the joint tenants." Once the original owner no longer has an interest, the whole property is generally reassessed.
  • Adding someone as a tenant in common is a change in ownership unless an exclusion applies, and only the portion that changes hands is reassessed. If half the property is transferred, the assessor reassesses half.

Transfers between parents and children have their own rules under Prop 19, which I walk through in my Prop 19 article. A Preliminary Change of Ownership Report is normally filed whenever a deed is recorded, and that's how the assessor learns about the change.

Can my lender call my loan due if I add someone to title later?

For most homeowners adding a spouse or child, no. Federal law bars lenders from enforcing a due-on-sale clause on homes with fewer than five units for several family transfers, including one where the borrower's spouse or children become an owner, a transfer on the death of a joint tenant, a transfer to a relative after the borrower's death, and a transfer into a living trust where the borrower stays a beneficiary (12 U.S.C. 1701j-3(d)).

Adding a sibling, friend or partner who isn't your spouse isn't on that list. Talk to your servicer and an attorney before you record that kind of deed. Being on title also isn't the same as being on the loan, a difference I covered for parents in my guide to helping an adult child buy.

Sometimes that's fine. Often it isn't what they'd have chosen with a few minutes of explanation, especially married couples who'd benefit from community property, or siblings who put in different amounts and assumed their share would go to their own kids. Frequently asked questions

What is the most common way for married couples to hold title in California?

Married couples typically choose among community property, community property with right of survivorship, and joint tenancy. Community property with right of survivorship combines automatic transfer to the surviving spouse with community property status, but the deed has to say so expressly. An attorney or tax professional can help you choose.

What happens to a joint tenancy home when one owner dies?

The surviving joint tenants receive the deceased owner's share without probate. They usually record an Affidavit of Death of Joint Tenant with a certified death certificate and a Preliminary Change of Ownership Report at the county recorder's office.

Should siblings buying together use joint tenancy or tenants in common?

It depends on what they want to happen if one of them dies. Joint tenancy requires equal shares and passes a deceased owner's share to the survivors. Tenants in common allows unequal shares and lets each owner leave their share to their own heirs. Tenancy in common is California's default when the deed doesn't say otherwise.

Will adding my spouse to my deed raise my property taxes?

Generally no. The California State Board of Equalization lists transfers between spouses, including adding a spouse to a deed, as excluded from reassessment. Registered domestic partners have a similar exclusion.

Can I change how I hold title after I buy?

Yes, by recording a new deed, but changes can have property tax, income tax and mortgage consequences. Check with an attorney, and see the Board of Equalization's rules on what counts as a change in ownership.
