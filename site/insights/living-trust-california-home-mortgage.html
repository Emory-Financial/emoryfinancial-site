Putting Your California Home in a Living Trust? What Happens to Your Mortgage, Your Property Taxes and Your Next Refinance

By Derek Shao, NMLS 242117 · November 20, 2026 · 8 min read

The short answer: For most California homeowners, moving your house into a revocable living trust does not let your lender call the loan due, as long as you remain a beneficiary and keep living there. Federal law specifically protects that transfer. It also isn't a "change in ownership" for California property tax purposes, so your assessed value stays put. The real work is in the details: how the deed is worded, telling your insurer and lender, and making sure the trust is set up so you can still refinance or open a HELOC later.

As of November 20, 2026. California probate thresholds cited apply to deaths on or after April 1, 2025, per the Judicial Council of California. This is general education, not legal advice; your estate planning attorney should draft and record your trust documents.

Why is this coming up now?

Because Thanksgiving is next week, and the end of the year is when a lot of families finally have "the conversation." In my work, it's one of the most common questions I hear from parents in their 60s and 70s: "My attorney says to put the house in a trust. Will that mess up my mortgage?" It's also a question I hear from adult children after a parent passes, when they discover the house never made it into the trust at all.

The good news is that the rules here are clearer than most people expect.

Can my lender call my loan due if I put my house in a living trust?

Not if the trust is set up the usual way. Almost every mortgage has a due-on-sale clause, which lets the lender demand full payment if you transfer the property. But the federal Garn-St Germain Act carves out specific transfers that a lender may not use as a reason to call the loan, for homes with fewer than five units.

One of those is "a transfer into an inter vivos trust in which the borrower is and remains a beneficiary and which does not relate to a transfer of rights of occupancy in the property" (12 U.S.C. § 1701j-3(d)(8)). In plain English: if you put your home into your own living trust, you're still a beneficiary, and you're not handing the right to live there to someone else, the lender can't accelerate the loan because of the transfer.

One caution: the protection is for a trust you're a beneficiary of. Deeding the house to an irrevocable trust for your kids, or to an LLC, is a different situation, and you should talk to your attorney and lender first.

Will putting my home in a trust trigger a property tax reassessment?

No, if it's a revocable trust. California's Revenue and Taxation Code § 62(d) excludes from "change in ownership" a transfer into a revocable trust, or into an irrevocable trust where the person transferring the property is a present beneficiary. The State Board of Equalization puts it simply: if a trust doesn't meet one of those tests, "there will be a change in ownership whenever property is transferred into a trust" (BOE Annotation 220.0760).

The tax question that does matter comes later. When the trust becomes irrevocable at death and the house passes to your children, that is generally a change in ownership, and whether your kids keep your low assessed value depends on Prop 19. I walk through those rules in my guide to inheriting a parent's California home.

Why bother with a trust at all? What about probate?

Mostly to keep the house out of probate. California does have simpler procedures for smaller estates, and they got more generous recently. For deaths on or after April 1, 2025, heirs can use a streamlined court petition for the decedent's primary residence if it's worth $750,000 or less, under AB 2016. The small-estate limit for other assets is $208,850 (California Courts Self-Help; Judicial Council of California).

Here's the catch for most of the families I work with: the median home in Los Angeles County alone was $955,090 in October, according to the California Association of Realtors. Plenty of California homes are worth more than $750,000, which means those homes can't use the streamlined petition. That's a big reason attorneys recommend a trust here. The courts' own guidance notes that property held in a living trust isn't counted toward these limits at all.

Can I still refinance or get a HELOC if my home is in a trust?

Usually, yes, but the trust has to be set up in a way lenders accept. Fannie Mae's rules are a good benchmark because many lenders follow them. Under its Selling Guide, the trust must be established by one or more individuals, the person who created it must be the primary beneficiary, the trustee must include that person (or an institutional trustee) and must "have the power to mortgage the security property," and at least one person who established the trust has to be a borrower. For a primary residence, at least one of them must live in the home and sign the loan documents (Fannie Mae Selling Guide B2-2-05). The title insurer also has to be comfortable insuring the loan without exceptions for the trust, so expect the lender or title company to ask for a copy or a certification of trust.

In practice, a few things trip people up:

  • The trust doesn't give the trustee power to borrow. Most do, but older or do-it-yourself trusts sometimes don't.
  • Some lenders and HELOC programs ask you to deed the home out of the trust and back in. It's a paperwork step, not a problem, but it adds time. Ask before you apply.
  • Successor trustees can't sign for you while you're alive and well. If you're the trustee, you sign.

If you're thinking about tapping equity in the next year or two, my HELOC vs. cash-out refinance guide covers the bigger decision.

What should I do, step by step?

The step people skip most often is the one after a refinance. Some lenders require the home to come out of the trust to close, and nobody puts it back. Years later, the family finds out the house has to go through probate anyway.

What happens to the mortgage when I pass away?

The loan doesn't disappear, but your heirs don't have to pay it off on the spot. Transfers to a relative after a borrower's death are protected under the same federal law, so the lender can't call the loan just because the house passed to your kids. And under the CFPB's mortgage servicing rules, people who receive a home after the death of a relative or through certain trusts can be confirmed as "successors in interest" and then generally get the same servicing protections as the original borrower (CFPB). In practice, that means a confirmed successor can work with the servicer much as the original borrower could.

Your heirs still have to keep the payments current, and if they want the loan in their own names, or want to keep the house and buy out a sibling, that usually means a new loan.

Derek\'s take

A living trust is one of the most useful things a California homeowner can do for their family, and your mortgage shouldn't stand in the way. Federal law protects the transfer, and California doesn't reassess you for it. Where I see trouble is in the follow-through: a house that came out of the trust for a refinance and never went back in, a trust that doesn't let the trustee borrow, or heirs who don't know who services the loan. Frequently asked questions

Will my lender call my loan due if I put my house in a living trust?

Not if it's a trust you remain a beneficiary of and the transfer doesn't hand occupancy to someone else. Federal law, 12 U.S.C. § 1701j-3(d)(8), bars lenders from enforcing a due-on-sale clause for that transfer on homes with fewer than five units.

Does transferring my home into a revocable trust trigger a property tax reassessment in California?

No. Revenue and Taxation Code § 62(d) excludes transfers into a revocable trust, or into an irrevocable trust where you are a present beneficiary, from change in ownership. A reassessment question can arise later, when the home passes to heirs.

Can I refinance or get a HELOC if my home is in a trust?

Usually. Fannie Mae allows loans to inter vivos revocable trusts when the person who created the trust is a borrower and primary beneficiary and the trustee has power to mortgage the property. Some lenders ask you to deed the home out of the trust to close, so make sure it goes back in afterward.

Do I need a trust if my home is worth less than $750,000?

Not necessarily. For deaths on or after April 1, 2025, California lets heirs use a simpler court petition for a primary residence worth $750,000 or less. Many homes in Los Angeles and coastal counties are worth more, and a trust also helps with other assets and incapacity, so talk to an estate planning attorney.

What happens to my mortgage when I die?

The loan stays in place. A transfer to a relative after a borrower's death is protected from due-on-sale enforcement, and heirs who are confirmed as successors in interest generally get the same servicing protections as the original borrower. They still need to keep the payments current.
