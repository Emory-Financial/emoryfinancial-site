Builders Are Cutting Prices and Buying Down Rates. Should California Buyers Take a Fresh Look at New Construction?

By Derek Shao, NMLS 242117 · November 18, 2026 · 7 min read

The short answer: Homebuilders are the most motivated sellers in the market right now. Builder confidence fell to its lowest level since April 2012 this month, 46% of builders cut prices and 68% offered sales incentives, according to the National Association of Home Builders. A big part of those incentives is paying down the buyer's mortgage rate, and Realtor.com found new-home buyers started at noticeably lower average rates than resale buyers in the third quarter. If you're open to new construction in California, now is a good time to look, as long as you compare the builder's offer against an outside lender and check the costs that come with newer communities, like Mello-Roos taxes and HOA dues.

As of November 18, 2026. Rates cited are published national averages, not rates offered by Emory Financial.

Why are builders suddenly so eager to deal?

Because buyers have stepped back. The NAHB/Wells Fargo Housing Market Index fell four points to 25 in November, the weakest reading since April 2012. Current sales conditions dropped to 28, and the gauge of prospective buyer traffic fell to 16, the lowest in 15 years outside the first months of the pandemic (NAHB, November 17, 2026).

NAHB's chief economist, Robert Dietz, put it simply: "Mortgage rates above 7%, softer consumer confidence and an uncertain economic outlook are holding buyers back." Builders in the West are the gloomiest in the country. The West's three-month average fell to 23, the lowest of the four regions.

Here's the part that matters to a buyer:

Are new-home buyers really getting lower rates?

On average, yes. A Realtor.com new-construction report released November 17 found that buyers of new homes had an average starting mortgage rate of 5.01% in the third quarter, compared with 6.15% for buyers of existing homes. Looking only at 30-year fixed loans, the gap was smaller: 6.19% for new homes versus 6.68% for existing homes (Fast Company, reporting on the Realtor.com report, November 2026). I couldn't open Realtor.com's report directly, so these figures come from that coverage.

Notice that the "all loans" gap is much wider than the 30-year fixed gap. That's a clue: some of what makes new-home rates look so low is temporary buydowns and other structures where the starting rate isn't the rate you'll pay for 30 years. Realtor.com senior economist Joel Berner made the same distinction, noting that a temporary buydown "can provide relief early on," which is different from a lower rate locked for the life of the loan.

For context, Freddie Mac's national weekly average for a 30-year fixed loan was 7.11% as of November 12 (Freddie Mac PMMS).

What's the difference between a temporary and a permanent buydown?

This is the single most important question to ask a builder's sales office.

  • Permanent buydown: The builder pays discount points so your rate is lower for the life of the loan.
  • Temporary buydown: The builder funds an account that covers part of your interest for the first one to three years. A common version is a "2-1": your effective rate is two percentage points below the note rate in year one, one point below in year two, then the full note rate from year three on.

Fannie Mae's rules for temporary buydowns are worth knowing because they protect you:

  • The lender "must qualify the borrower based on the note rate without consideration of the bought-down rate." In plain English, you have to be able to afford the full payment, not just the year-one payment.
  • The buydown period can't exceed three years, and the part of the rate you pay can't rise more than 1% in any one-year interval.
  • They're allowed on primary homes and second homes, not investment properties.
  • The buydown must be a written agreement between you and whoever is paying for it.

(Fannie Mae Selling Guide B2-1.4-04)

Do I have to use the builder's lender to get the deal?

Often the best incentives are tied to the builder's affiliated or preferred lender. That can be a good deal, but it's still worth checking. The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau's advice is direct: "Even if a real estate agent or homebuilder has a preferred lender, still look for a better deal" (CFPB, Contact multiple lenders). The CFPB also notes that within a 45-day window, multiple lenders can check your credit without additional impact on your score.

The way to compare is to ask for two things in writing: the builder's total incentive in dollars if you use their lender, and what's left if you don't. Then compare full Loan Estimates side by side, not just the headline rate.

One more rule to know: under Fannie Mae's guidelines, if a builder or its affiliated lender pays for a buydown, that cost counts toward the cap on what an "interested party" can contribute, generally 3% to 9% of the price on a conventional loan depending on your down payment (Fannie Mae Selling Guide B3-4.1-02). I broke down those caps in How Buyers Can Use Seller Credits Right Now.

What California costs come with a new home?

Newer California communities often carry costs that a 1960s ranch house down the street doesn't. Budget for them before you fall in love with the model home.

  • Mello-Roos special taxes. Many new developments sit inside a community facilities district. Tulare County describes Mello-Roos as "a special assessment imposed on those real property owners within a Community Facilities District," levied on your property tax bill to repay bonds for roads, schools and other public improvements (Tulare County, What is Mello-Roos?). Sellers of one-to-four-unit homes must make a good-faith effort to give you the district's special tax notice (California DRE, Disclosures in Real Property Transactions). Ask for it early, because your lender counts it in your monthly housing cost.
  • Supplemental property tax bills. In California, buying triggers a reassessment, and the state's required notice warns that "you may receive one or two supplemental tax bills" that "are not mailed to your lender" (same DRE booklet).
  • HOA dues. Many new communities have an HOA, and the dues count toward your debt-to-income ratio. If it's a condo or townhome, read the reserve study (see California's New HOA Reserve Law).
  • Insurance. Some new subdivisions are at the edge of town, closer to wildfire risk. Get an insurance quote before you sign the purchase contract, not after.

What should I do before I walk into a sales office?

If you're watching California's Prop 37 down payment loans for new homes, keep in mind that the program isn't open yet, as I explained in Voters Approved Prop 37 and Prop 1. Today's builder incentives are available now and don't depend on it.

Derek\'s take

I've watched a lot of buyers rule out new construction because the list prices looked high. In this market that's worth a second look. Builders can't sell homes to buyers who don't show up, and right now they're paying real money to get you to the closing table. The catch is that a builder incentive is easy to make look bigger than it is. A temporary buydown that saves you money for two years is not the same as a lower rate for thirty, and a "free" upgrade package doesn't help you qualify. My job is to put the builder's offer next to an outside one, in dollars, so you can see which is actually better. If you're touring model homes this winter, book a call with me before you sign anything. Thirty minutes, no credit pull, no obligation.

Frequently asked questions

Are builders really cutting prices right now?

Yes. In November 2026, 46% of builders surveyed by the National Association of Home Builders cut prices, the highest share since the pandemic began, and 68% offered sales incentives. The typical price cut has held at 6% for eight straight months.

Is a builder's 2-1 buydown a good deal?

It can be, but it only lowers your rate for the first two years. Under Fannie Mae's rules, the lender has to qualify you at the full note rate, so make sure you're comfortable with the payment in year three and beyond, and compare it with a permanent buydown or a price cut.

Do I have to use the builder's lender?

You can choose your own lender, but the builder may offer bigger incentives if you use theirs. The CFPB recommends shopping anyway. Ask the builder what the incentive is with and without their lender, and compare full Loan Estimates.

What is Mello-Roos, and will I pay it on a new home?

It's a special tax levied in a community facilities district to repay bonds for local improvements, and it appears on your property tax bill. Many newer California developments have one. The seller is required to make a good-faith effort to give you the district's disclosure notice.

Does a lower builder rate mean the house costs less overall?

Not necessarily. New homes often have higher asking prices, and buydowns are part of what the builder is spending to make the deal work. Compare the total cost, including price, rate for the full loan term, closing costs, Mello-Roos and HOA dues.
