Divorce and the Family Home in California: Keep It, Sell It, or Buy Out Your Spouse?

By Derek Shao, NMLS 242117 · October 16, 2026 · 5 min read

The short answer: In California, a home bought during the marriage is generally community property and is divided equally. If one of you keeps it, the usual route is a refinance that pays out the other spouse's share. Fannie Mae treats that buyout as a limited cash-out refinance, often with better pricing than a regular cash-out. A quitclaim deed changes who owns the house, not who owes the loan, so the refinance is what actually takes your ex off the mortgage. Transfers between spouses in a divorce don't trigger a Prop 13 reassessment.

Who owns the house in a California divorce?

California is a community property state. What you own or owe together during the marriage is generally divided equally by the judge, and a mortgage taken out during the marriage is community debt that you're both responsible for (California Courts self-help). A home one spouse owned before the marriage can stay separate property, but only as long as it was kept separate. Paying down that mortgage with income earned during the marriage can give the other spouse a claim. That's a question for your family law attorney.

What are the options for the house?

There are three, and each has a different financing picture:

  1. One spouse keeps it and buys out the other. The most common path when there are children in school or a low property tax base worth keeping.
  2. Sell and split the proceeds. The cleanest break, and often the right answer when neither spouse can qualify alone.
  3. Keep owning it together for a set period. Sometimes used until the youngest child finishes school. It keeps both of you on the loan and tied together financially, so it needs a very clear written agreement.

How does a spousal buyout work?

Fannie Mae treats buying out a co-owner under a divorce as a limited cash-out refinance, not a regular cash-out, when (Fannie Mae B2-1.3-02):

  • you've owned the home jointly for at least 12 months before the new loan,
  • there's a written agreement stating the terms of the transfer and how the money is used, and
  • the spouse keeping the home doesn't take any of the proceeds themselves.

The spouse who keeps the home must qualify on their own income. That's often the hardest part, and it's where planning ahead pays off.

Can alimony or child support help me qualify?

Yes, if it's documented. The lender needs proof of full, regular, on-time payments for the most recent 6 months, and the payments must continue for at least 3 years from the application date (Fannie Mae B3-3.4-02). If your support ends when your youngest turns 18 in two years, it generally can't count. Tell your attorney early if you'll need support income to qualify; the timing and wording of the agreement matter.

Can't I just quitclaim the house to my spouse?

A quitclaim deed changes ownership, not liability. If your ex keeps the house and you stay on the loan, a late payment hurts your credit, and that payment counts against you when you try to buy your next home. Federal law does keep the lender from calling the loan due when a divorce decree transfers the home to a spouse (12 U.S.C. 1701j-3), but the original borrower isn't released unless the lender approves it (Fannie Mae Servicing Guide D1-4.1-02). In practice, a refinance is what gets the departing spouse off the loan.

What about property tax and capital gains?

  • Property tax: Transfers between spouses, including those under a divorce settlement or court order, are excluded from reassessment automatically (California BOE). The spouse who keeps the home keeps the existing Prop 13 base.
  • Transfer between spouses: Under federal tax law, no gain or loss is recognized on a transfer to a spouse or former spouse that's incident to the divorce, and the receiving spouse keeps the original tax basis (26 U.S.C. 1041).
  • Selling later: The home-sale exclusion is up to $250,000 of gain for a single filer. A divorce doesn't erase years of ownership: the IRS lets the receiving spouse count the time the other spouse owned the home, and an owner can count time an ex-spouse lived there under the divorce agreement (IRS Publication 523).

Have your CPA run the numbers before you agree on a value. A home with a large built-in gain is worth less to the spouse who keeps it than the same dollar amount in cash.

Frequently asked questions

Is the house automatically split 50/50 in California?

Community property is generally divided equally, but how that happens, whether through a buyout, a sale or an offset against other assets, is up to you, your attorneys and the court.

How long do we need to have owned the home for a buyout refinance?

Fannie Mae's limited cash-out treatment requires at least 12 months of joint ownership before the new loan funds.

Will keeping the house reset my property taxes?

No. Transfers between spouses under a divorce are excluded from reassessment in California.

Can I count child support as income?

Yes, with 6 months of documented receipt and at least 3 years of payments remaining.
