Home for Thanksgiving and Wondering if Mom and Dad Should Get a Reverse Mortgage? What California Families Should Know First

By Derek Shao, NMLS 242117 · November 26, 2026 · 9 min read

The short answer: A reverse mortgage lets a California homeowner 62 or older turn part of their home equity into cash without making monthly mortgage payments, but interest and fees are added to the balance every month, so the debt grows and the equity shrinks. Your parents still have to pay property taxes and homeowners insurance and keep the house up, or they can face foreclosure. California adds its own protections, including required counseling and a seven-day wait before a lender can take the application. It can be a good fit for a parent who wants to stay put and has a cash-flow problem, and a poor fit for one who may move soon or wants to leave the house free and clear.

As of November 26, 2026. Loan limits and premiums are from HUD; California rules are from the Civil Code and the State Controller. HUD hadn't announced its 2027 reverse mortgage limit as of today.

Why is this coming up at Thanksgiving?

Because this is often the one weekend a year the whole family is in the same kitchen. I hear some version of the same story every December: a parent who is house-rich and cash-poor, a property tax bill due December 10, a roof that needs replacing, and a sibling who saw a reverse mortgage ad and wants to know if it's a scam. It isn't a scam, but it is a big decision with long-term consequences for your parents and, later, for you. The best time to talk about it is before anyone is under pressure.

What exactly is a reverse mortgage?

Almost all reverse mortgages today are Home Equity Conversion Mortgages, or HECMs, insured by the Federal Housing Administration. The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau describes it as "a special type of home loan only for homeowners who are 62 and older" (CFPB).

The key difference from a regular mortgage or HELOC is the direction of the payments. Your parents don't make a monthly payment. Instead, "interest and fees are added to the loan balance each month and the balance grows," and "as your loan balance increases, your home equity decreases" (CFPB).

Money can come out in a few ways (CFPB, Considering a Reverse Mortgage):

  • A line of credit, where you "only pay interest on money you use," and the amount available grows over time.
  • Monthly payments, which the CFPB says can make sense "if you need additional monthly income to cover daily living expenses."
  • A single lump sum, which the CFPB notes "typically offer[s] less money than other HECM payout options."

There are limits on how much can be drawn in the first year.

How much home value can a reverse mortgage count?

HUD caps the home value it will count, called the maximum claim amount. For 2026, HUD raised it from $1,209,750 to $1,249,125 for FHA case numbers assigned on or after January 1, 2026, and the same limit applies everywhere in the country (HUD, December 11, 2025). That 2026 figure matched FHA's high-cost ceiling for regular one-unit loans.

For 2027, FHFA has already raised the high-cost conforming ceiling to $1,283,100 (FHFA, November 24, 2026), but as of today HUD had not published its 2027 HECM limit. I'll update this when it does. This matters in much of coastal California, where plenty of longtime owners' homes are worth more than the cap. A home worth more than the limit doesn't get extra borrowing power from the excess value.

What does it cost?

The biggest piece most families don't expect is FHA mortgage insurance. Under HUD's current premium structure, the upfront premium is "two percent (2.00%) of the Maximum Claim Amount," and the annual premium is "one-half of one percent (0.50%) of the outstanding mortgage balance," which is added to the balance along with interest (HUD Mortgagee Letter 2017-12). On top of that come the usual closing costs, origination charges and servicing. Ask for a written breakdown and compare more than one lender.

That mortgage insurance does buy something real: the CFPB notes that "neither you nor your heirs will have to pay back more than your home is worth" (CFPB).

What do my parents still have to pay?

This is the part that causes the most trouble. A reverse mortgage removes the mortgage payment, not the cost of owning a home. The CFPB says homeowners "are required to pay property taxes and homeowners insurance" and keep the house in good condition, and warns: "You could face foreclosure if you run out of money to pay property taxes, insurance, or other housing-related expenses in the future" (CFPB; CFPB guide).

In California right now, that's not a small caveat. Insurance premiums have climbed sharply, and some older owners have been pushed onto the FAIR Plan. Before anyone signs, I'd want to see that your parents can keep paying taxes, insurance and any HOA dues for the long haul, not just this year.

What protections does California add?

California's Civil Code layers several safeguards on top of the federal rules:

  • A plain-language warning first. Before counseling, the lender must give the borrower an "IMPORTANT NOTICE TO REVERSE MORTGAGE LOAN APPLICANT" in large type, saying they're required to consult an independent counselor (Cal. Civ. Code § 1923.5).
  • A worksheet to bring to counseling. The borrower gets a "Reverse Mortgage Worksheet Guide" with questions to discuss. It must be signed and returned, and the application can't be approved until it is (§ 1923.5).
  • Counseling and a seven-day wait. The lender must provide a list of at least 10 HUD-approved counseling agencies, and it can't accept the application or charge fees until seven days after counseling (Cal. Civ. Code § 1923.2).
  • No annuity strings. A lender can't require the borrower to buy an annuity as a condition of the loan or steer them to buy one before closing (§ 1923.2).

After closing, the CFPB notes that most reverse mortgages come with a three-day right to cancel (CFPB).

What happens when a parent passes away or moves out?

The loan "must be repaid when the last borrower, co-borrower or eligible spouse sells the home, moves out of the home, or dies," and the CFPB notes that "most people need to sell their home to pay off the loan" (CFPB guide). Heirs who want to keep the home can pay the loan off, often by refinancing, and that's where planning ahead pays off. If you're also thinking about keeping your parents' low property tax base, read my guide to Prop 19 and inheriting a parent's home, because the move-in deadlines are tight.

Two family situations to get right up front:

  • A spouse or partner who isn't on the loan. The CFPB says that if your parent lives with a spouse or partner, "it usually makes sense to apply as co-borrowers." Someone living in the home who isn't a co-borrower or an eligible spouse may have to move out or repay the loan when the borrower leaves or dies.
  • An adult child living at home. Same issue. A caregiver son or daughter who lives there has no right to stay once the loan comes due.

If your parents' home is in a living trust, the lender will need to review the trust. My piece on living trusts and mortgages covers how that usually works.

What else should we compare it with?

A reverse mortgage isn't the only way to free up cash. Depending on the problem, these may fit better:

The Property Tax Postponement program is worth a look if the problem is mostly the tax bill. It's run by the State Controller for homeowners who are 62 or older, blind or disabled, with at least 40% equity and household income of $55,181 or less (the limit set for the 2025–26 cycle). Postponed taxes accrue interest at 5% a year and are repaid later, and applications are taken from October 1 to February 10 each year. One catch: "An applicant with an existing reverse mortgage is not eligible," and taking a reverse mortgage later makes postponed taxes due (State Controller, PTP FAQ). Check the Controller's site for the current year's income limit before applying.

If you're thinking about stepping in to help with money yourself, my guide to helping family with a gift or co-signing covers the tax side.

Derek\'s take

I'm not for or against reverse mortgages. I've seen one keep a widowed parent in the home she loved with real breathing room, and I've seen families surprised years later by how much the balance grew. The ones that work have three things in common: the parent plans to stay a long time, the family has talked openly about what happens to the house later, and there's a realistic plan for taxes and insurance. If this came up around your table this week, sit in on the counseling session with your parents, ask for every cost in writing, and compare it honestly with a HELOC, property tax postponement or downsizing. I'm happy to walk your family through the options, even if the answer is that a reverse mortgage isn't the right tool. Book a call with me: thirty minutes, no credit pull, no obligation.

Frequently asked questions

How old do you have to be to get a reverse mortgage in California?

For a federally insured HECM, the homeowner must be 62 or older, live in the home as a principal residence and keep paying property taxes, homeowners insurance and upkeep.

Can my parents lose their home with a reverse mortgage?

Yes, if they fall behind on property taxes, insurance or other housing costs, or stop living in the home. The loan also comes due when the last borrower or eligible spouse sells, moves out or dies.

Do heirs owe more than the house is worth?

No. With a HECM, the CFPB says neither the borrower nor the heirs will have to repay more than the home is worth. Heirs who want to keep the house generally need to pay off the loan, often by refinancing; otherwise the home is usually sold.

Is there a waiting period in California?

Yes. Under California Civil Code § 1923.2, a lender can't accept a reverse mortgage application or charge fees until seven days after the borrower completes counseling with an independent counselor.

Can you have a reverse mortgage and use California's Property Tax Postponement program?

No. The State Controller says an applicant with an existing reverse mortgage isn't eligible, and taking a reverse mortgage while in the program makes the postponed taxes due.
