Buy, Remodel, or Tear Down and Rebuild? A California Homeowner's Decision Guide

By Derek Shao, NMLS 242117 · October 16, 2026 · 4 min read

The short answer: If you love your location and have a low mortgage rate, remodeling usually wins, financed with a HELOC or renovation loan so you keep your first mortgage. If the house itself is the problem (layout, foundation, size) and the lot is valuable, rebuilding can make sense but takes the most cash, time and patience. If the neighborhood or school district no longer fits, buying is the cleaner answer, even at a higher rate.

The three paths at a glance

Buy a different home Remodel what you have Tear down and rebuild
Typical timeline 2–4 months 3–12 months 18–36 months including design and permits
Keep your current mortgage rate? No Yes, with a HELOC or second Usually no; construction financing replaces it
California property tax (Prop 13) Reassessed on the full purchase price Only the added value of the new construction is assessed; land and existing house keep their base The new house gets a new assessed value; the land generally keeps its base year value
Where you live during the work No disruption Often at home, or a short-term rental Rental for 12+ months
Cost certainty High: price known at contract Medium: surprises behind walls Lowest: bids, permits and change orders
Selling costs Roughly 5–7% of the sale price None None

The property-tax row is where California differs most. Under Prop 13, assessors add value only for the new construction, while the land and untouched structure keep their indexed base (Sacramento County Assessor; Santa Cruz County Assessor). Owners who bought decades ago often save thousands a year by staying.

Option 1: Buy a different home

Pros: Fastest path to the house you want. Known price. No construction risk.

Cons: You give up your current rate and your Prop 13 base, and pay selling costs. Homeowners 55 and older may be able to transfer their tax base under Prop 19, which can change the math entirely.

Financing: Conventional loans up to $1,249,125 in Los Angeles County for 2026 (FHFA via Calculated Risk); jumbo above that. Need to buy before you sell? Ask about a bridge loan or HELOC on your current home for the down payment.

Option 2: Remodel what you have

Pros: Keep your neighborhood, rate and tax base. Spend only on what's missing.

Cons: Living in a construction zone. Costs can drift. Over-improving for the street limits resale value.

Financing options:

  • HELOC: draw as you go; keeps your first mortgage untouched. Best when you have equity today.
  • Fixed home equity loan: one lump sum at a fixed rate; predictable.
  • Fannie Mae HomeStyle Renovation: borrow against the after-renovation value; renovation funds up to 75% of the as-completed value (Fannie Mae). Useful when your current equity is thin.
  • FHA 203(k): Limited 203(k) now covers up to $75,000 in non-structural repairs; Standard 203(k) handles larger and structural projects (HUD comparison).
  • Cash-out refinance: only when your current rate is already near today's rates.

Option 3: Tear down and rebuild

Pros: Exactly the house you want on land you already love. New systems, energy efficiency, and often more square footage.

Cons: The longest timeline and largest budget. Carrying a rental and a construction loan at the same time. City approvals vary widely across the San Gabriel Valley.

Financing options:

  • Construction-to-permanent (one-time close): one loan funds the build in draws, then converts to a regular mortgage.
  • Two-step construction loan: a short-term construction loan, then a separate mortgage at completion. More flexible, with two sets of closing costs.
  • Land equity counts. If you own the lot free and clear or with low debt, that equity often covers the down payment.

Checklist: which path fits you?

Count your "yes" answers in each group.

Lean toward buying if:

  • You want a different school district, commute or neighborhood
  • Your current mortgage rate is close to today's rates
  • You need the space within six months
  • You or your spouse are 55+ and Prop 19 lets you carry your tax base

Lean toward remodeling if:

  • You love the location and the lot
  • Your rate is well below today's
  • The house is structurally sound and the layout mostly works
  • The project is under roughly a third of your home's value

Lean toward rebuilding if:

  • The lot is worth more than the house on it
  • The foundation, layout or systems need replacing anyway
  • You can live elsewhere for 12–24 months
  • You have substantial equity or cash reserves

Derek\'s take

The most expensive mistake I see is choosing the path first and the financing second. A 15-minute call before you hire an architect or a listing agent can save you a year. I'll run all three scenarios side by side with your actual numbers.

Frequently asked questions

Does remodeling raise my California property taxes?

Only on the value the new construction adds; the rest keeps its existing base.

Can I get a renovation loan with little equity?

Yes. HomeStyle and 203(k) lend against the after-renovation value.

Is a HELOC or a cash-out refinance better for a remodel?

If your first-mortgage rate is low, a HELOC usually wins because it leaves that rate in place.

How much cash do I need to rebuild?

Typically enough for design, permits and a construction-loan down payment, plus 12–24 months of rent. Land equity can offset much of it.
