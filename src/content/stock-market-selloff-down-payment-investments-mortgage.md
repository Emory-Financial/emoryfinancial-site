**The short answer:** If you're buying in the next few months and part of your down payment is in stocks or mutual funds, last week was a reminder that the money isn't fully yours until it's in cash. Lenders do accept investments for a down payment, but they count them at the statement value net of any margin loan, and if the account isn't worth at least 20% more than what you need to bring, you'll have to show the shares were sold and the cash landed. The same selloff pushed Treasury yields down sharply, which may give mortgage rates some relief, but it doesn't change the Fed's December meeting.

*As of November 23, 2026. Market figures are as reported on the dates noted. Mortgage rates cited are Freddie Mac national averages, not rates offered by Emory Financial.*

## What happened in the markets last week?

The S&P 500 fell 8.6% for the week ending November 20, its worst week since April 2025, and closed more than 11% below its October 6 record, which puts it in correction territory. The Nasdaq fell about 10% and the Dow about 8%. The selloff was led by AI stocks ([VestGen, November 23, 2026](https://www.vestgenim.com/2026/11/23/sp-500-enters-correction-as-ai-selloff-spreads/); [Breitbart, November 20, 2026](https://www.breitbart.com/economy/2026/11/20/stocks-plunge-as-ai-selloff-pushes-sp-500-and-dow-into-correction/)).

When stocks fall that hard, money tends to move into Treasury bonds, and bond yields drop. That's what happened. The 10-year Treasury yield, which mortgage rates loosely follow, fell from 5.15% on November 13 to 4.92% on November 20, according to the U.S. Treasury's daily yield data ([U.S. Treasury](https://home.treasury.gov/resource-center/data-chart-center/interest-rates/TextView?type=daily_treasury_yield_curve&field_tdr_date_value_month=202611)).

## Does that mean mortgage rates are coming down?

Maybe a little, but don't count on it yet. Freddie Mac's weekly survey put the national average 30-year fixed rate at 7.28% as of November 19, up from 7.11% a week earlier and 6.26% a year ago, and that survey was taken before Friday's biggest drop in yields ([Freddie Mac PMMS, November 19, 2026](https://www.freddiemac.com/pmms)). The next survey comes out after Thanksgiving week.

The bigger picture hasn't changed. Futures markets were still pricing in roughly 89% odds of a quarter-point Fed hike at the December 9 decision as of the end of last week, and this week brings October's PCE inflation report on Wednesday, November 25 ([Mortgage Daily, November 22, 2026](https://www.mortgagedaily.com/rates/mortgage-rates-week-ahead-2026-11-22/)). If you're in contract, this is a conversation to have with your loan officer about your lock, not a reason to gamble.

## Can I use stocks or mutual funds for my down payment?

Yes. Fannie Mae's guidelines say vested stocks, government bonds and mutual funds "are acceptable sources of funds for the down payment, closing costs, and reserves provided their value can be verified" ([Fannie Mae Selling Guide B3-4.3-01](https://selling-guide.fanniemae.com/sel/b3-4.3-01/stocks-stock-options-bonds-and-mutual-funds)). A few details matter more in a falling market:

- **Value is net of margin.** The lender counts the account value "net of any margin accounts," usually from your most recent monthly or quarterly statement. If you've borrowed against your portfolio, that loan comes off the top.
- **Your statement can be stale.** A statement dated before last week's drop shows money you may no longer have. What you actually bring to closing is what the shares sell for on the day you sell them.
- **Unvested stock doesn't count.** Fannie Mae says non-vested stock options "are not an acceptable source of funds for the down payment, closing costs, or reserves." That matters to a lot of tech employees here in California.

## What is the 20% rule?

It decides whether you have to prove you sold. Under Fannie Mae's rule, if the value of your stocks or funds is at least 20% more than what you need for the down payment and closing costs, the lender doesn't need to document that you sold them. If it isn't, you have to show "evidence of the borrower's actual receipt of funds realized from the sale or liquidation."

Here's a hypothetical. Say you need $150,000 at closing for your down payment and costs, and that money is in a brokerage account. Under the rule, the account needs to be worth at least $180,000 for you to skip the proof-of-sale paperwork. If a selloff knocks it below that line, you'll need to sell and show the cash arriving. And if it drops below $150,000, you're short no matter what.

## Should I sell now or wait for the market to bounce back?

That's an investment decision, and I'm not your financial advisor. But here's how I'd think about it from the mortgage side. Money you'll need for a down payment in the next few months is short-term money, and short-term money and stocks don't mix well. Nobody knows whether last week was the bottom. What I do know is that a closing date doesn't move because the market had a bad week.

If you do sell, a few practical things help:

- **Leave time for settlement.** Trades take time to settle and transfers take time to clear. Don't plan to sell the week of closing.
- **Keep the paper trail.** Save the trade confirmation and the statement showing the cash moving to your bank. Your loan officer will likely ask for both.
- **Think about taxes first.** Selling in a taxable account can trigger capital gains, or let you realize a loss. Talk to your tax preparer before December 31 if the timing matters to you.

## What about my 401(k) or IRA?

Fannie Mae accepts vested retirement funds for the down payment, closing costs and reserves, as long as the lender confirms the account is vested and "allows withdrawals regardless of current employment status." For reserves, Fannie Mae "does not require the funds to be withdrawn" ([Fannie Mae Selling Guide B3-4.3-03](https://selling-guide.fanniemae.com/sel/b3-4.3-03/retirement-accounts)).

Pulling the money out is a different story. The IRS charges an additional 10% tax on most early distributions. The first-time homebuyer exception covers up to $10,000, and it applies to IRAs, not 401(k)s ([IRS, exceptions to tax on early distributions](https://www.irs.gov/retirement-plans/plan-participant-employee/retirement-topics-exceptions-to-tax-on-early-distributions)). Ordinary income tax can still apply.

A 401(k) loan is another route, if your plan offers one. Fannie Mae treats money borrowed against financial assets like a 401(k) or stocks as "a return of equity," and says payments on loans secured by your financial assets "do not have to be considered as long-term debt" when you qualify ([Fannie Mae Selling Guide B3-4.3-15](https://selling-guide.fanniemae.com/sel/b3-4.3-15/borrowed-funds-secured-asset)). The catch: if you also count that account as reserves, its value is reduced by what you borrowed. Your plan's rules on repayment if you leave your job matter too.

## FAQ

**Can I use stocks for a down payment on a house?** Yes. Fannie Mae accepts vested stocks, bonds and mutual funds for the down payment, closing costs and reserves, valued from your most recent statement net of any margin loan. If the account is worth less than 120% of what you need at closing, you'll have to document that you sold and received the cash.

**Do I have to sell my stocks before closing?** Not always. If the account is worth at least 20% more than your down payment and closing costs, Fannie Mae doesn't require proof of sale. You still need cash at closing, though, so in practice you'll sell before then. Stocks counted only as reserves don't have to be sold.

**Does a stock market drop affect my mortgage approval?** It can. If your down payment or reserves are in investments and their value falls, you may come up short of what the lender verified. Tell your loan officer early if your account value changes meaningfully.

**Can I use my 401(k) for a down payment?** Yes, vested 401(k) funds are acceptable to Fannie Mae. A withdrawal may trigger income tax and the IRS's 10% additional tax, and the $10,000 first-time homebuyer exception applies to IRAs, not 401(k)s. A 401(k) loan avoids that tax, and its payments generally don't count in your debt-to-income ratio under Fannie Mae's rules.

**Will mortgage rates drop because stocks fell?** They may ease a little. The 10-year Treasury yield fell from 5.15% to 4.92% between November 13 and November 20, and mortgage rates loosely follow it. But the Fed's December 9 decision and this week's inflation data could push them back up.
