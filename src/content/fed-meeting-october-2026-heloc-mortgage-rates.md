**The short answer:** The Federal Reserve meets October 27–28 and announces its decision Wednesday, six weeks after it raised its benchmark rate for the first time in more than three years. A Fed move flows almost directly into HELOCs and other prime-based lines of credit, but it doesn't set 30-year fixed mortgage rates, which follow the bond market and were already near 7.3% on average. If you have a HELOC balance, look at your rate and payment now; if you're buying, focus on your rate lock rather than trying to time the Fed.

## What did the Fed do in September, and what's expected this week?

On September 16, 2026, the Fed raised the target range for the federal funds rate by a quarter point, to 3.75%–4.00%, on a 12–0 vote, saying "inflation remains elevated" ([Federal Reserve, September 16, 2026](https://www.federalreserve.gov/newsevents/pressreleases/monetary20260916a.htm)). It was the first increase in more than three years ([U.S. Bank, September 16, 2026](https://www.usbank.com/investing/financial-perspectives/market-news/federal-reserve-interest-rate.html)).

Inflation hasn't cooled since. Consumer prices rose 3.7% over the 12 months through September, with energy up 20.4% and shelter up 3.2% ([Bureau of Labor Statistics, October 14, 2026](https://www.bls.gov/news.release/archives/cpi_10142026.htm)). In the minutes of the September meeting, most Fed officials judged that "another increase in the target range for the federal funds rate would likely be appropriate" by year end ([FOMC minutes, September 15–16, 2026](https://www.federalreserve.gov/monetarypolicy/fomcminutes20260916.htm)). Governor Christopher Waller said on October 8 that he anticipates additional hikes, though not necessarily at back-to-back meetings ([Federal Reserve, October 8, 2026](https://www.federalreserve.gov/newsevents/speech/waller20261008a.htm)).

So this week could bring a hold or another quarter-point increase. Either way, the Fed's next scheduled meeting after this one is December 8–9 ([Federal Reserve meeting calendar](https://www.federalreserve.gov/monetarypolicy/fomccalendars.htm)). I'm not going to guess the outcome. What matters more is knowing which of your loans actually responds.

The Fed sets a short-term, overnight rate. The loans that feel it fastest are the ones tied to short-term benchmarks:

- **The prime rate**, which was 7.00% on October 22 ([Federal Reserve H.15](https://www.federalreserve.gov/releases/h15/)). Banks generally adjust prime when the Fed changes its target, so it's the most direct link between a Fed decision and your household.
- **Fixed-rate mortgages** follow longer-term bond yields and expectations about inflation, not the Fed's overnight rate. On October 22, the 10-year Treasury yield was 4.96% and Freddie Mac's national average for a 30-year fixed was 7.32%, down from 7.40% the week before but well above 6.19% a year earlier ([Freddie Mac PMMS, October 22, 2026](https://www.freddiemac.com/pmms)). The 15-year average was 6.68%.

Fed officials noticed this too: in September's minutes, staff noted that mortgage rates rose a bit more than 10-year Treasury yields, and a few officials said housing was a sector where financial conditions "did not appear supportive of activity." I covered how rates climbed this fall in [Mortgage Rates Climbed Past 7.4% This Fall](/insights/mortgage-rates-7-4-percent-california-fall-2026).

## I have a HELOC. What happens to my payment?

Most HELOCs have a variable rate made of two parts: an index plus a margin. The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau lists the U.S. prime rate as one of the most common indexes, and warns that with a variable rate, "your monthly payments may change even if you don't draw more money" ([CFPB HELOC booklet](https://files.consumerfinance.gov/f/documents/cfpb_heloc-brochure.pdf)).

A lot of Southern California homeowners opened HELOCs in the last few years to remodel, add an ADU or consolidate debt without giving up a low first mortgage. That was often the right call, and it still can be. But if your line follows prime, a Fed hike typically shows up on your statement soon after prime changes. Three things to check this week:

1. **Your index and margin.** They're on your HELOC agreement or monthly statement.
2. **Whether you're in the draw period or the repayment period.** Interest-only payments during the draw period feel any rate change right away. When repayment starts, payments jump because you're paying principal too.
3. **Whether you can fix part of the balance.** The CFPB notes some HELOCs let you convert part of your balance to a fixed rate. It usually starts higher than the variable rate but makes your payment predictable.

If you're weighing a HELOC against a cash-out refinance, my [HELOC vs. cash-out refinance guide](/insights/heloc-vs-cash-out-refinance) walks through the blended-rate math.

## I'm buying or refinancing. Should I lock before Wednesday?

Mortgage rates often move on what the Fed *says* as much as on what it does, so the statement and Chair Warsh's press conference can move bond yields in either direction. Nobody can promise which way. My practical advice:

- **If you're in escrow and your rate is reasonable for your budget, lock it.** A lock protects you from the swing either way, as long as you close on time and your application doesn't change.
- **If you're still shopping homes,** get fully pre-approved so you can lock quickly once you're in contract. Don't let one meeting decide whether you buy.
- **If you're thinking about refinancing a fixed-rate loan,** the Fed meeting alone shouldn't change your math. What matters is the gap between your current rate and today's rates, plus your costs and how long you'll stay.

**Reading from outside California?** Fed decisions and Freddie Mac averages are national, so all of this applies to you too. Prime-based HELOCs work the same way across the country.

## FAQ

**Does the Fed set mortgage rates?** No. The Fed sets a short-term target for the federal funds rate. Fixed mortgage rates follow longer-term bond yields, such as the 10-year Treasury, and investors' expectations about inflation.

**When will the Fed announce its decision?** The meeting runs October 27–28, 2026, and the statement is released Wednesday afternoon, October 28. The next scheduled meeting is December 8–9.

**Will a Fed hike raise my HELOC rate?** If your HELOC is tied to the prime rate, very likely. Banks generally move prime when the Fed changes its target, and your rate is prime plus your margin. Check your agreement for the index, margin and any caps.

**Does a Fed hike change my existing fixed-rate mortgage?** No. A fixed rate stays the same for the life of the loan. Your escrow payment can still change if your property taxes or insurance change.

**Should I wait for the Fed to cut rates before I buy?** The Fed raised rates in September and officials have signaled more increases are possible, so waiting for cuts is a guess. If the home and the payment fit your budget, focus on locking a rate you're comfortable with.
