**The short answer:** If your mortgage payment includes taxes and insurance, an escrow shortage letter means your account will run short of what your servicer needs to pay those bills and keep the allowed cushion. Federal rules limit that cushion to one-sixth of a year's escrow bills, and if the shortage is one month's escrow payment or more, your servicer can't demand it all at once: it must either let it ride or spread it over at least 12 months. With FAIR Plan premiums up an average 29.1% since October 15 and 2026–27 property tax bills arriving now, this is the season to read that letter line by line, check the math, and decide whether to pay the shortage up front or spread it out.

*As of October 31, 2026. Rules cited are federal (RESPA's Regulation X) and California law; your servicer's letter and loan documents control the details.*

## Why are so many escrow payments going up right now?

Two of the biggest bills your escrow account pays are changing at the same time.

**Insurance.** On October 15, 2026, the California FAIR Plan's average rate increase of 29.1% took effect for new and renewing policies, down from the 35.8% the plan had requested. Homes in high wildfire-risk areas will pay the most, and some lower-risk policyholders will see decreases ([KQED, August 11, 2026](https://www.kqed.org/news/12094860/california-fair-plan-announces-29-1-rate-hike-for-homeowners-this-fall)). If you're on a FAIR Plan policy plus a separate Difference in Conditions (DIC) policy, both premiums may be coming out of escrow. I walked through the FAIR Plan change in detail in [the FAIR Plan's 29% rate increase and your mortgage payment](/insights/california-fair-plan-rate-increase-mortgage).

**Property taxes.** Los Angeles County is mailing about 2.4 million 2026–27 secured property tax bills by November 1. The first installment is due November 1 and goes delinquent after close of business December 10, 2026 ([LA County Treasurer and Tax Collector, October 1, 2026](https://content.govdelivery.com/attachments/CALACOUNTY/2026/10/01/file_attachments/3801832/Press%20Release.pdf)). If your taxes went up, your servicer will pay the higher bill and then catch up on your escrow. Here's [what to check on your LA County tax bill before December 10](/insights/la-county-property-tax-bill-2026-27-deadlines).

The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau puts it simply: because property taxes and insurance premiums can change from year to year, the escrow part of your payment, and your total monthly payment, can change too ([CFPB](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/ask-cfpb/what-is-an-escrow-or-impound-account-en-140/)).

## What's the difference between a shortage, a deficiency and a surplus?

These three words show up on escrow statements, and they mean different things under Regulation X ([12 CFR 1024.17](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/12/1024.17)):

- **Shortage:** your balance will fall short of the *target* balance the servicer is allowed to keep, including the cushion. You're not in the red, you're just below where the account should be.
- **Deficiency:** your account balance is *negative*. The servicer has already paid more out than you put in.
- **Surplus:** you've paid in more than needed.

The servicer's options depend on which one you have and how big it is compared with one month's escrow payment:

Two details worth knowing. The surplus refund rule applies if you're current, which the regulation defines as the servicer receiving your payments within 30 days of the due date. And your servicer must send you an annual escrow statement within 30 days of the end of each escrow computation year ([12 CFR 1024.17(i)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/12/1024.17)).

## How much extra can my servicer hold?

The cushion, the extra money held in reserve for surprises, can be no more than one-sixth of the estimated total annual payments from the account, roughly two months' worth ([12 CFR 1024.17(c)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/12/1024.17)). Your loan documents can allow less, but not more.

This matters because a big premium jump hits you twice. Your servicer recalculates the cushion on the new, higher bills, and it recovers any shortage on top of that.

**A hypothetical, for scale:** say your escrow analysis shows a $1,200 shortage and the servicer spreads it over 12 months. That's $100 a month added to your payment for the coming year, *on top of* the higher ongoing monthly escrow amount that reflects your new insurance and tax bills. If you pay the $1,200 shortage up front, you avoid the $100, but the higher ongoing escrow amount stays. Your own numbers will differ.

## Should I pay the shortage up front or spread it out?

There's no single right answer, but here's how I think about it:

- **Pay it up front** if you have the cash in savings and want the lowest possible monthly payment this year. Many servicers include a coupon or an online option to do this; check your letter for the deadline and instructions.
- **Spread it out** if cash is tight or you'd rather keep your reserves. When the shortage is one month's escrow payment or more, the 12-month minimum is your right under federal rules, not a favor.
- **Either way,** remember that next year's payment may drop slightly once the shortage is repaid, but only if your taxes and premiums stop climbing.

Before deciding, check the letter.

## What if I think the escrow math is wrong?

Mistakes happen, especially after a policy change. The usual suspects: a new insurance policy was added but the old one wasn't removed, the servicer projected a premium higher than your actual renewal, or a supplemental tax bill you already paid yourself was counted again.

The CFPB's advice is to contact your servicer right away. If that doesn't fix it, you can send a written information request or a notice of error, which is a letter disputing the mistake. Watch for warning signs like a payment change without notice that your taxes or insurance changed, force-placed insurance you didn't ask for, or word from the county that your taxes weren't paid. If taxes weren't paid, send your servicer a copy of the tax bill with a notice of error and contact the tax collector right away ([CFPB](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/ask-cfpb/what-should-i-do-if-im-having-problems-with-my-escrow-or-impound-account-en-2082/)). You can also file a complaint with the CFPB at consumerfinance.gov/complaint or (855) 411-2372, or get free help from a HUD-approved housing counselor or the HOPE Hotline at (888) 995-4673.

## Can I get rid of my escrow account in California?

Sometimes. California Civil Code section 2954 generally bars a lender from *requiring* an impound account as a loan condition on a single-family, owner-occupied home, but with important exceptions. A lender may require one when ([Civil Code § 2954](https://california.public.law/codes/civil_code_section_2954)):

- a state or federal regulator requires it, or the loan is made, guaranteed or insured by a government agency (FHA and VA loans, for example);
- you've failed to pay two consecutive tax installments before the delinquency date;
- the original loan was 90% or more of the sale price or appraised value, or combined loans exceed 80% of the appraised value;
- the loan is a "higher-priced mortgage loan" under federal Regulation Z, or was refinanced or modified under a homeownership preservation program.

If none of those apply, ask your servicer what it takes to waive escrow; investor rules and fees vary. But dropping escrow doesn't make the bills smaller. You'd be writing two large tax checks a year and paying your insurance directly, and the CFPB warns that a lapse can lead to force-placed insurance that usually costs more ([CFPB](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/ask-cfpb/what-is-an-escrow-or-impound-account-en-140/)). For most of my clients, escrow is a budgeting tool worth keeping.

## FAQ

**Why did my mortgage payment go up if my interest rate is fixed?** Your fixed rate covers principal and interest. If you have an escrow account, your payment also includes property taxes and insurance, which can change every year. When they rise, your servicer raises the escrow part of your payment.

**Does my servicer have to let me pay an escrow shortage over 12 months?** If the shortage is one month's escrow payment or more, federal rules say the servicer may either let it ride or have you repay it in equal monthly payments over at least 12 months. It can't demand the full amount at once. For smaller shortages, it can ask for repayment within 30 days.

**How big a cushion can my lender keep in escrow?** No more than one-sixth of the estimated total annual payments from the account, about two months' worth, under Regulation X. Your loan documents can set a lower limit.

**Will I get a refund if I overpaid escrow?** If the annual analysis shows a surplus of $50 or more and you're current on your payments, the servicer must refund it within 30 days. Under $50, it can refund it or credit it toward next year.

**Can I cancel my impound account in California?** Possibly. California Civil Code § 2954 generally keeps lenders from requiring impounds on single-family, owner-occupied homes, but there are exceptions, including government-backed loans, loans at 90% or more of the home's value, combined loans over 80%, and higher-priced mortgage loans. Ask your servicer about its waiver rules.
