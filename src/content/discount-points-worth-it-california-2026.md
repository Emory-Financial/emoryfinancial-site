**The short answer:** Discount points are an upfront fee, usually 1% of the loan amount per point, that you pay at closing in exchange for a lower interest rate for the life of the loan. They only pay off if you keep the loan past your break-even point, the number of months it takes the savings to repay what you spent. With Freddie Mac's national 30-year average back up to 7.28% this week and the Fed signaling more hikes, points are tempting, but if there's a real chance you'll refinance or sell in the next few years, the cash may do more for you elsewhere.

## What exactly are discount points?

The CFPB defines them simply: "Points lower your interest rate, in exchange for paying more at closing." One point equals 1% of the loan amount, so one point on a $400,000 loan costs $4,000. Lenders also price in fractions, like a quarter or three-eighths of a point ([CFPB](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/ask-cfpb/how-should-i-use-lender-credits-and-points-also-called-discount-points-en-136/)).

The reverse also exists. Lender credits lower your closing costs up front in exchange for a higher rate, and a lender's worksheet may call them "negative points."

Two details matter when you're comparing offers:

- **Points show up on page 2, Section A of your Loan Estimate.** By law, points listed there must be connected to a discounted interest rate.
- **There's no fixed exchange rate.** The CFPB notes that "discount points have no fixed value in terms of the change in interest rate." One lender's point might buy a bigger rate cut than another's, which is why the CFPB says to ask every lender for the same amount of points or credits when you compare.

## How many buyers are paying points?

More than used to. When rates climbed from 2021 to 2023, the CFPB found the share of homebuyers paying points roughly doubled, and nearly 9 out of 10 cash-out refinance borrowers paid them. Among those who paid, the median was about 1 point on a purchase and 2.1 points on a cash-out refinance ([CFPB Data Spotlight, April 2024](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/data-research/research-reports/data-spotlight-trends-in-discount-points-amid-rising-interest-rates/)).

The CFPB was skeptical about how well that worked out. Then-Director Rohit Chopra said the heavy use of points "suggests that many borrowers are uncertain about their ability to refinance in the future." The agency also cited Freddie Mac research finding the rate difference between borrowers who paid points and those who didn't was "minor" from 2018 to 2023, though that comparison doesn't fully control for differences between borrowers.

## How do I know if points will pay off for me?

Work out your break-even. The CFPB's rough method is to divide the cost of the points by your monthly savings. The answer is the number of months before the points have paid for themselves. Keep the loan longer than that and you come out ahead. Sell or refinance sooner and you've lost money.

That makes the real question how long you'll keep this particular loan, not how long you'll live in the house. The CFPB says points are "less useful for cash-strapped borrowers and those who expect to refinance or move in the near future." With rates well above where they were a year ago, plenty of today's buyers hope to refinance if rates come down. If you're one of them, every month you shave off the life of the loan makes points harder to justify.

## Can the seller pay my points?

Often, yes, and in this market that's worth asking for. Points are one of the things a seller credit can pay for, within the limits your loan type sets. I covered the limits in my [seller credits guide](/insights/california-buyers-leverage-seller-credits-fall-2026), and builders are doing the same thing with [rate buydowns on new construction](/insights/new-construction-builder-rate-buydowns-california-2026).

The same break-even logic applies even when it's the seller's money. A credit toward your closing costs or a temporary buydown may help you more than permanent points if you expect to refinance.

## Are mortgage points tax deductible?

Sometimes, and the rules differ for purchases and refinances ([IRS Topic 504](https://www.irs.gov/taxtopics/tc504)):

- **Buying your main home:** if you itemize, you can generally deduct points in full in the year you pay them, but only if you meet all of the IRS conditions. Among them, the loan must be to buy, build or improve your principal residence and be secured by it, the points must be a percentage of the loan and show clearly as points on your settlement statement, and you must bring at least that much of your own money to closing, not money borrowed from the lender or broker.
- **Refinancing or a second home:** points are generally deducted a little at a time over the life of the loan.
- **Seller-paid points:** the IRS treats them as if you paid them, as long as you subtract them from your cost basis in the home.

Fees like appraisal, notary and mortgage insurance aren't deductible as points. Not everyone itemizes, so ask your tax preparer whether the deduction would actually help you before counting it in your math.

## FAQ

**What is one mortgage point?** One point equals 1% of your loan amount, paid at closing to lower your interest rate. On a $400,000 loan, one point costs $4,000. How much the rate drops varies from lender to lender.

**How do I calculate the break-even on mortgage points?** Divide the cost of the points by the monthly savings from the lower rate. The result is how many months it takes for the points to pay for themselves. If you're likely to sell or refinance before then, the points cost you money.

**Are points a good idea if I plan to refinance later?** Usually not. Points pay off over time, so refinancing early cuts that time short. The CFPB says points are less useful for borrowers who expect to refinance or move in the near future.

**Can I deduct mortgage points on my taxes?** If you itemize and meet the IRS conditions, points on a loan to buy your main home can generally be deducted in the year you pay them. Points on a refinance are generally deducted over the life of the loan. Check with a tax professional.

**What are lender credits?** The opposite of points. The lender pays some of your closing costs in exchange for a higher interest rate. They can make sense if you expect to keep the loan only a short time or need to save cash at closing.
