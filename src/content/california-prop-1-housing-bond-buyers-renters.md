**The short answer:** Proposition 1 on California's November 3, 2026 ballot would let the state borrow $11.25 billion through bonds for housing programs. According to the Legislative Analyst's Office (LAO), $7.2 billion would go to affordable rental housing, $1.1 billion to homeownership help for low- and moderate-income first-time buyers, and $1.25 billion to the veterans' home loan program. The official estimate is a state cost of $500 million to $600 million a year for about 25 years, paid from the existing budget rather than a new tax. Who would qualify for the buyer help depends on income limits and program rules that would be set later.

*As of October 23, 2026. This article explains the measure; it does not recommend a vote.*

## What would Prop 1 actually do?

Prop 1 asks voters to approve two state general obligation bonds, which are loans the state repays over time:

- **A $10 billion bond** for state housing programs, repaid from the state's General Fund (the main state budget).
- **A $1.25 billion bond** for veterans' housing, repaid by veterans' home loan payments. The LAO says that part has no direct state cost, because veterans' payments have always covered that debt.

The state has used bonds for housing for decades. The LAO notes that voters approved $1 billion for the veterans home loan program in 2018 and $2 billion in 2024 for housing veterans and people with mental health or substance use challenges facing homelessness.

The LAO estimates the bond could help fund:

- **Up to 40,000 affordable rental units**
- **Up to 40,000 households with homeownership help**, including about **2,100 veterans**
- **About 2,500 farmworker units** and **about 1,200 university student beds**

## If I'm hoping to buy my first home, would Prop 1 help me?

Possibly, if your income fits. The official voter guide describes the $1.1 billion homeownership piece as help for **"low- and moderate-income households,"** used two ways:

1. **Homebuying assistance for first-time buyers**, through loans that generally aren't repaid until the home is sold or the mortgage is paid off. In lender language, that's a deferred, or "silent," second loan: no monthly payment, but you pay it back later.
2. **Money for nonprofits and other groups** to buy land for homes for eligible low-income buyers, and to repair homes already owned by low-income households.

What the voter guide does **not** spell out: specific income limits, maximum assistance per buyer, or which state programs would run the money. Those details would come later. So if you're a first-time buyer in Los Angeles County, where prices are high and "moderate income" is defined county by county, it's too early to know how many local buyers would qualify.

One practical point from my side of the desk: a deferred second loan still has to fit your first mortgage lender's rules, and it's repaid when you sell or refinance. That matters when you plan a refinance down the road.

## How is Prop 1 different from Prop 37?

They're separate measures, and either, both or neither could pass.

- **Prop 1** is a general obligation bond for many housing programs, repaid from the state budget (plus the self-funded veterans piece).
- **Prop 37** would let CalHFA sell revenue bonds and lend middle-income buyers up to 17% of the price of a **newly built** home, repaid by those buyers' monthly payments. I walked through it in [Prop 37 on California's November Ballot](/insights/california-prop-37-new-home-down-payment-loan).

## What does Prop 1 cost, and what do each side say?

**The cost:** The official estimate is **$500 million to $600 million a year for about 25 years**, which the LAO puts at about 0.25% of the state's General Fund budget each year. Because the state borrows and repays over time, the LAO says the total cost is about 15% more, after adjusting for inflation, than paying for the programs up front. Prop 1 does not raise taxes on its own; repayment comes from the existing budget.

**Supporters** (the official ballot argument; CalMatters lists Gov. Gavin Newsom, the California Democratic Party, the California Apartment Association and AFL-CIO California among them) say it builds affordable homes for veterans, seniors, people with disabilities and low- and middle-income working families, dedicates $1.25 billion to veterans, and comes with "strict accountability and public reporting" and "no new taxes."

**Critics:** No official argument against Prop 1 was submitted for the voter guide. CalMatters reports that some Republican state lawmakers and the Orange County Register oppose it. Their arguments: the state shouldn't borrow more for housing without fixing "the underlying reasons" homes are so expensive to build, and the measure uses veterans to build support while doing little for them.

## What else on the November ballot touches housing?

A few other statewide measures are worth a quick look if you own, buy or rent:

- **Prop 45** would change California's environmental review law (CEQA) to speed up review of many housing, transportation, water and health projects and set deadlines for resolving lawsuits. Supporters say it cuts red tape that makes California unaffordable; opponents say it limits environmental and public review of projects in your community.
- **Prop 42** would bar new state taxes on owning personal property, which the measure defines as everything people own **other than real estate**. So it is not about your home's property tax bill.
- **In the City of Los Angeles**, Prop TE affects some Palisades Fire homeowners who sell. See [LA's Prop TE on the November Ballot](/insights/la-prop-te-palisades-fire-mansion-tax).

**Outside California?** Prop 1's programs are for California housing. If you're moving here or helping family buy here, the details to watch after the vote are income limits and first-time buyer rules.

## FAQ

**When is the vote on Prop 1?** Tuesday, November 3, 2026, in California's statewide general election.

**Would Prop 1 raise my taxes?** It doesn't create a new tax. The official estimate is that repaying the $10 billion bond would cost the state's General Fund $500 million to $600 million a year for about 25 years, money that would come from the existing budget. The $1.25 billion veterans bond is repaid by veterans' loan payments.

**How much of Prop 1 goes to helping people buy homes?** The LAO lists $1.1 billion for homeownership (first-time buyer assistance, land for low-income buyers' homes, and repairs for low-income owners), plus $1.25 billion for veterans' home loans. Farmworker housing money can also go to owner-occupied homes.

**What are the income limits for Prop 1 down payment help?** The voter guide says "low- and moderate-income households" but doesn't set specific limits. Those would be set by the state programs that run the money if the measure passes.

**Is Prop 1 the same as Prop 37?** No. Prop 1 is a general obligation bond for several housing programs. Prop 37 is a separate, self-funded loan program for buyers of new homes. They are separate votes.
